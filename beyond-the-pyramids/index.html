Field Notes

The Investigation

I am a student investigating how Egypt can better protect its cultural heritage while using it to support tourism and economic growth. I chose this question because the Giza Pyramids and Grand Egyptian Museum are central to both Egypt’s identity and economy. Here, “better management” includes restoration, ticketing reform, new visitor and museum infrastructure, digital cataloguing and public-private partnerships.

This investigation asks three questions: what was going wrong, what changed, and did the reforms work? I use tourism and revenue data, field observations, interviews with Egyptians connected to the sites, and international comparisons to assess their impact and economic trade-offs.

Exhibit I

Why were the Pyramids and Egyptian Museum struggling before management reforms?

Before recent reforms, both the Giza Pyramids and the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir faced problems that limited their cultural and economic potential. Ageing infrastructure, overcrowding and weak visitor facilities affected the tourist experience, while informal vendors and fragmented site management made areas around the Pyramids difficult to regulate. Outdated ticketing and administrative systems also created opportunities for revenue leakage, meaning Egypt was not always capturing the full economic value generated by millions of visitors.

These weaknesses became more severe after the political instability of 2011, when international tourism to Egypt fell dramatically. With fewer visitors and reduced tourism revenues, maintaining and modernising heritage sites became more difficult. Together, these problems highlighted a wider challenge: Egypt possessed some of the world’s most valuable cultural assets, but the way they were managed was limiting both their preservation and their economic value.

Economic Question

What happens when a country possesses globally valuable cultural assets, but fails to manage them efficiently?

Poor management creates an opportunity cost: lost tourism revenue, weaker investment and fewer resources for conservation. The challenge for Egypt was therefore not only to protect its heritage, but to capture more of the economic value it could generate.

Exhibit II

What changed?

Egypt’s response went beyond simply building a new museum. Heritage management increasingly combined large-scale infrastructure investment with conservation and restoration, improved digital systems, modern visitor facilities and new approaches to museum operations. The Grand Egyptian Museum became the centrepiece of this shift, designed not only to display artefacts but also to improve their preservation, research and long-term management while strengthening Egypt’s tourism economy.

2002 — A new museum takes shape

The foundation stone for the Grand Egyptian Museum was laid near the Giza Pyramids, beginning the development of a purpose-built alternative to the ageing and overcrowded Egyptian Museum in Tahrir.

2010 — Conservation becomes central

The GEM Conservation Center opened, providing specialised facilities for conserving, restoring and studying Egyptian artefacts. Japanese-Egyptian cooperation also brought training, technology and new conservation practices to the project.

2016 — Investment accelerates

Japan approved a second JICA loan of ¥49.409 billion for GEM, following the ¥34.838 billion first loan agreed in 2006. Together, JICA's two loans provided approximately ¥84.2 billion (around US$800 million) for construction, exhibitions, ICT infrastructure and related services.

2025 — The Grand Egyptian Museum opens

After the main galleries began welcoming visitors on a trial basis in 2024, the Grand Egyptian Museum officially opened on 1 November 2025, marking the culmination of decades of investment in a new model for displaying, conserving and managing Egyptian heritage.

Economic Question

Why invest hundreds of millions of dollars in cultural heritage when the economic return may take decades to materialise?

Heritage investment creates significant upfront costs, while many of its benefits emerge over the long term. Better museums and heritage sites can attract visitors, encourage tourism spending and private investment, create employment, and generate resources that can support future conservation. The economic judgement therefore depends on whether these long-term private and social benefits outweigh the opportunity cost of committing scarce resources today.

Exhibit III

Did it work?

The results point to a significant transformation. Egypt is attracting record numbers of tourists while investment in museums and archaeological sites has expanded conservation capacity and improved the way visitors experience its heritage. However, rising tourism cannot be attributed to heritage reform alone: exchange rates, security, air connectivity, marketing and wider tourism investment also influence demand.

The Verdict

So, did it work?

The evidence suggests substantial progress. Egypt reached a record 19 million tourists in 2025, while museum and archaeological-site admissions and conservation capacity also increased. These results suggest that Egypt is extracting greater economic value from its heritage while investing more heavily in its preservation. But they do not prove that management reform alone caused the tourism boom. The strongest conclusion is that heritage reform has become one important part of a much wider transformation of Egypt's tourism economy.

Economic Question

Do rising tourist numbers prove that heritage-management reforms worked?

Economic Analysis

Not by themselves. The figures show correlation, but establishing causation is more difficult because tourism demand is influenced by security, exchange rates, global travel conditions, marketing, air connectivity and other investment. Stronger evidence comes from examining several outcomes together. Record tourism alongside higher heritage-site admissions and measurable improvements in conservation provides a more convincing case that the reforms have generated both economic and cultural benefits.

Framework

The Economic Lens

Heritage reform is also an economic decision. How sites are funded and managed affects incentives, resource allocation and who gains from tourism. Six economic concepts reveal the benefits — and trade-offs — behind Egypt’s reforms.

Incentives

Better services can generate greater revenue.

Private operators have a commercial incentive to improve visitor services because a better experience can attract visitors and spending. Government faces a broader incentive: generating economic value while protecting cultural assets for the future.

Case Evidence Private-sector visitor services at Giza

Multiplier Effect

Tourist spending travels beyond the museum gate.

Visitors also spend on hotels, restaurants, transport, guides and local businesses. Heritage tourism can therefore generate additional income and employment throughout the wider economy.

Externalities

More tourism creates benefits — and costs.

Conservation preserves heritage for future generations and tourism can support nearby employment. But overcrowding, congestion and pressure on archaeological sites create costs, so maximising visitor numbers does not necessarily maximise social welfare.

Efficiency

Better management can make the same assets work harder.

Digital systems, organised transport and specialist management can reduce inefficiencies and improve the visitor experience. But long-term efficiency must include preservation, not simply maximising short-term revenue.

Case Evidence Organised transport + modern visitor systems

Equity

Who actually gains from the reforms?

Tourism growth creates opportunities for workers, businesses and government. Yet commercialisation raises a distributional question: whether local communities and Egyptians receive a fair share of the benefits generated by their national heritage.

Opportunity Cost

¥84.2 billion could have been used elsewhere.

Resources committed to the GEM could have supported alternative priorities. The investment is economically justified only if its long-term benefits — including conservation, tourism income and employment — outweigh the value of those alternatives.

Case Evidence ≈¥84.2bn in JICA financing

Evidence: JICA; Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities; State Information Service.

Weighing the Evidence

The Trade-offs

Heritage-led growth is not costless. The same reforms that can improve conservation, visitor experience and tourism revenue can also create new pressures. The question is therefore not whether reform produces benefits, but whether those benefits are achieved without undermining accessibility, authenticity or the heritage itself.

Voices of Egypt

Data can show what changed, but not how those changes affected the people who experienced them. I spoke with people who experienced Egypt's tourism and heritage sector from different perspectives, using their first-hand accounts to test the findings presented throughout this investigation.

Visitor from the Gulf

“It was crammed up… it wasn't very organised, but this one is totally different. There's a clear experience and pathway and places to go.”

“Once you get in here, you realise how big it is.”

“It's a must-do. I recommend people spend an appropriate amount of time to cover all the experiences and halls.”

Why it matters

Fadi had visited the older Egyptian Museum as well as the GEM, giving a direct visitor comparison between the two experiences.

Harry Lewis

Visitor from England

“The museum is beautiful.”

“My Egyptian friends said the museum is really great and we should visit.”

“Of course. A hundred percent.”

Why it matters

Harry's visit shows how recommendations and word-of-mouth can make the GEM part of the decision to visit Egypt.

Michael Bertuzzi

Visitor from Canada · Previous visitor to the Egyptian Museum

“They're very different. This is obviously architecturally stunning, with lots and lots of space, displays and interpretive signage.”

“The old museum kind of was a museum in itself. It was interesting to see the artefacts, but there was none of this.”

“My parents were here in the winter, and they enjoyed the museum. We knew that if we did come, we were going to go snorkeling, see the Pyramids and, of course, the museum.”

Why it matters

Having visited the older museum around 26 years earlier, he provides a direct comparison between the previous museum experience and the GEM.

Grand Egyptian Museum Gift Shop Employee

“Visitors value the Egyptian handicrafts and appreciate that the products are made here in Egypt.”

“It brings the artefacts and the civilisation together in one place. People come from all over the world to see Egypt's history and civilisation.”

Why it matters

This perspective adds an employee view of what visitors value, including locally made products and the concentration of Egyptian heritage within the museum.

Industry Perspective

Findings

Policy Brief

The evidence suggests that Egypt should continue modernising heritage management, but not pursue tourism growth at any cost. Future policy should protect accessibility and conservation while ensuring that the economic benefits of heritage tourism are measured and shared more widely.

Curator's Note

About the Project

I chose this topic because Egypt's heritage is almost always discussed as history, and much more rarely as an economy. The Giza Pyramids and the Grand Egyptian Museum are studied as monuments and collections, yet they are also assets that have to be financed, staffed, maintained and managed — and the way they are managed determines both how well they are preserved and how much value they create for the people who live alongside them.

I wanted to test that idea with evidence rather than assumption, so this project combines tourism and financing data with primary sources from people who have worked directly on these sites. My aim was not to prove that reform succeeded, but to examine what changed, what the results actually show, and what was traded away in the process.

If there is one thing I hope a visitor takes from this investigation, it is that heritage management is an economic decision as much as a cultural one, and that the two goals are not automatically in conflict. Working on it has shown me how much of economics sits in exactly this kind of question: how scarce resources are organised, who benefits, and what is given up to achieve a result.
